<h2 id="ti-request">“We need a training”</h2>

<p>It arrives at 4:52 on a Thursday: “Hey, can L&amp;D put together a time management workshop for my team? Ideally before the end of the month.” One line. A format, a deadline and a hopeful emoji.</p>

<p>What it does not say is everything you need. Why now? What is going wrong? How many people? What would be different if the workshop worked? Most training requests arrive like this, as a solution looking for a problem. The fastest way to fix that is not a better course. It is a better set of questions, asked before anyone opens a design tool.</p>

<p>This guide gives you those questions: nine of them, plus a quick check on whether training is the right answer at all, and a simple way to decide what goes first.</p>
<h2 id="ti-what">What is a training intake form?</h2>

<p class="lo-def"><strong>A training intake form is the standard set of questions a business area answers before L&amp;D starts any work, so every request arrives with a problem, an audience and a measurable goal.</strong></p>

<p>It is the front door of your <a href="#blog-what-is-learning-operations">learning operation</a>. Without it, requests come in through email, chat, hallway conversations and calendar invites, each in a different shape. With it, every request becomes comparable, and comparable requests can be prioritized.</p>

<p>The pressure to get this right is real. In ATD’s 2026 State of the Industry report, prioritizing multiple demands is among the top three challenges for 46% of talent development teams, followed by scaling with few resources at 33% (<a href="https://www.td.org/content/atd-blog/2026-state-of-the-industry-report-indicates-latest-td-trends" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ATD</a>). Part of that load is self-inflicted: when a request arrives incomplete, three things go wrong.</p>

<ul class="pg-list"><li><strong>The wrong solution gets built.</strong> The request names a format before anyone has looked at the cause.</li><li><strong>Nobody can prioritize.</strong> Without impact or audience size, every request looks equally urgent.</li><li><strong>Nobody can report results.</strong> If no KPI and baseline were captured at the start, there is nothing to compare against at the end.</li></ul>
<h2 id="ti-questions">The 9 training intake questions</h2>

<p>Ask the requester to answer these before any design work starts. They fall into three groups: the problem, the people and the proof. The proof group is the one most requests skip, and it is the one you will need most when someone asks, months later, whether the training worked.</p>
<h3>The problem</h3>

<p><strong>1. What business problem are you trying to solve?</strong><br>Ask for what is happening today, not the training they want. “New hires take four months to handle calls on their own” is a problem. “We need onboarding videos” is a solution.</p>

<p><strong>2. What should people do differently on the job?</strong><br>Two or three observable behaviors that would show the problem is solved. These become the learning objectives and, later, what you evaluate.</p>

<p><strong>3. What happens if we do nothing?</strong><br>The cost, risk or opportunity lost. This answer separates real urgency from deadline pressure.</p>
<h3>The people</h3>

<p><strong>4. Who is the audience?</strong><br>Roles, number of people, regions, languages and shifts. Audience size feeds the priority score; the details shape format and logistics.</p>

<p><strong>5. What has already been tried, and what happened?</strong><br>Previous courses, job aids or process changes. It saves you from rebuilding something that already failed, and it often reveals the real cause.</p>

<p><strong>6. What constraints apply?</strong><br>Deadline, budget, compliance requirements, systems involved and who must approve the content. These shape scope before anyone promises a plan.</p>
<h3>The proof</h3>

<p><strong>7. Which business KPI will move if this works?</strong><br>Time to productivity, conversion rate, error rate, customer satisfaction, safety incidents. One KPI beats five.</p>

<p><strong>8. What is that KPI today, and where does the number come from?</strong><br>This is your baseline. Name the system or report that owns the number, so you can pull it again after the program.</p>

<p><strong>9. What target, by when, would count as success?</strong><br>A target and a date turn a wish into a commitment both sides can check: “from four months to ten weeks by Q3.”</p>
<p>A useful rule: if the requester cannot answer questions 1, 7 and 8, the request needs a short discovery call before it enters the queue. That is not bureaucracy. It is fifteen minutes that can save fifteen weeks.</p>
<h2 id="ti-triage">Is training the right answer? A triage checklist</h2>

<p>Training fixes gaps in knowledge and skill. If the cause is somewhere else, a course will not move the KPI, no matter how good it is. Before scoring a request, L&amp;D ticks each statement that is true:</p>

<ul class="pg-list"><li>People do not know how to do the task (not just that they are not doing it).</li><li>The tools, systems and information they need are already in place.</li><li>Expectations are clear, and people get feedback on their performance.</li><li>Incentives and workload do not work against the new behavior.</li><li>Managers will reinforce the behavior after the training.</li><li>The requester can share the KPI data before and after.</li></ul>

<div class="tbl"><table><thead><tr><th><strong>Ticks</strong></th><th><strong>What it means</strong></th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>5–6</td><td>Training is likely part of the answer. Move on to scoring.</td></tr><tr><td>3–4</td><td>Proceed, and name the non-training actions in the plan.</td></tr><tr><td>0–2</td><td>Recommend a process change, tool, job aid or communication instead.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>Saying “this is not a training problem” is one of the most valuable things L&amp;D can do for the business, as long as you explain why and suggest what would work instead. It also protects your team’s time for the requests where training really is the answer.</p>
<h2 id="ti-score">How to score and prioritize training requests</h2>

<p>Once a request passes triage, score each criterion from 1 (low) to 5 (high) and multiply it by its weight.</p>

<div class="tbl"><table><thead><tr><th><strong>Criterion</strong></th><th><strong>What a 5 looks like</strong></th><th><strong>Weight</strong></th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Business impact</td><td>Tied to a top company goal, with a measurable KPI</td><td>30%</td></tr><tr><td>Size of the audience</td><td>A large or critical population</td><td>20%</td></tr><tr><td>Risk of not acting</td><td>Compliance, safety or revenue at risk</td><td>20%</td></tr><tr><td>Readiness</td><td>Baseline data, a sponsor and subject matter experts available now</td><td>15%</td></tr><tr><td>Effort (inverted)</td><td>Light effort for L&amp;D: 5 = lightest</td><td>15%</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>The weighted total gives a recommendation:</p>

<ul class="pg-list"><li><strong>4.0 or higher:</strong> schedule now.</li><li><strong>3.0 to 3.9:</strong> add to the backlog and review at the next planning cycle.</li><li><strong>Below 3.0:</strong> suggest an alternative or decline, and share the reason with the requester.</li></ul>

<p><strong>An example.</strong> A sales onboarding request scores impact 5, audience 3, risk 4, readiness 4 and effort 3. The total is (5 × 0.30) + (3 × 0.20) + (4 × 0.20) + (4 × 0.15) + (3 × 0.15) = 3.95. It goes to the backlog, not the top of the queue, and the requester can see exactly why: better readiness or a lighter scope would move it up.</p>

<p>Adjust the weights to your organization, but publish them. A visible rule is far easier to defend than a decision made behind a closed door.</p>
<h2 id="ti-mistakes">Common training intake mistakes, and how to avoid them</h2>

<ul class="pg-list"><li><strong>Asking for a format instead of a problem.</strong> “Do you want e-learning or a workshop?” invites a solution before anyone understands the cause. Ask question 1 first, every time.</li><li><strong>Accepting a vague KPI.</strong> “Engagement” or “awareness” cannot be measured before and after. Push gently for a number the business already tracks.</li><li><strong>Skipping the baseline.</strong> A target without a starting point is a guess. If the number does not exist yet, agree on how to capture it before launch.</li><li><strong>Making the form too long.</strong> Nine questions is enough. Every extra field lowers the odds that a busy manager finishes it. Keep the form short and use a follow-up call for detail.</li><li><strong>Collecting answers nobody reads.</strong> An intake form only works if the answers drive a decision. Score every request the same way, and tell the requester what happens next and when.</li></ul>

<h2 id="ti-curiosity">A curiosity: the request that keeps coming back</h2>

<div class="lo-saros"><p>In <a href="#blog-what-is-learning-operations" style="color:#FF9A61">our guide to learning operations</a>, we told the story of the saros, the cycle of about 18 years and 11 days after which similar eclipses return. Babylonian astronomers found it by keeping records, not by staring harder at the sky.</p>

<p>Intake works the same way. One request for “a time management workshop” is a request. The same request from three teams in one year is a signal: maybe the problem is workload, or meeting culture, or a tool nobody was trained on. You only see that pattern if every request is written down in the same shape.</p>

<p>That is the quiet reason the nine questions matter. They do not just make one request better. Over time, they turn your inbox into a record you can read, and that idea is exactly where the name of our platform, Saros, comes from.</p></div>
