<h2 id="idm-model">The model comes before the tool</h2>

<p>The instructional design model decides what a course does before anyone writes a slide. Pick the wrong one and even good content misses the goal: a decision skill taught as a lecture, a quick fix built as a three-month program, a motivation problem treated as a content problem.</p>

<p>AI makes this choice more important, not less. It can draft a module in minutes, but it drafts whatever structure it is given. If nobody chooses the model first, the result reads well and teaches little.</p>

<p>This guide compares 12 models: ReFrame Learning, the proprietary method behind Saros, and 11 recognized models used across corporate learning. For each one you get what it is best for, when it fits and when to avoid it.</p>

<h2 id="idm-each">Each model in brief</h2>

<h3>ReFrame Learning</h3>
<p>The proprietary method behind Saros. It is built for one job: unblocking a specific performance bottleneck that the data has already shown, in four short steps. <strong>Use it</strong> when a team keeps failing at one step of a process. <strong>Avoid it</strong> when the goal is a broad program from scratch, or when nobody has the data yet: diagnose first.</p>

<h3>Gagné's 9 Events of Instruction</h3>
<p>Nine steps in a fixed order, from gaining attention to helping people retain what they learned. It is the classic recipe behind most structured corporate e-learning. <strong>Use it</strong> for modules with a clear objective that several instructors must deliver the same way. <strong>Avoid it</strong> for open exploration or creative work, where a fixed sequence feels rigid.</p>

<h3>Merrill's First Principles</h3>
<p>Five principles found in every effective course, with a real-world task at the center. It is a set of principles, not a recipe. <strong>Use it</strong> to design task-centered training or to review a course you already have. <strong>Avoid it</strong> as the only guide when stakeholders want a ready step-by-step structure.</p>

<h3>4MAT</h3>
<p>A cycle that answers four questions in order: why, what, how and what if. It serves analytical, practical and imaginative learners in the same room. <strong>Use it</strong> for mixed audiences and train-the-trainer programs. <strong>Avoid it</strong> in microlearning of a few minutes: the full cycle needs time.</p>

<h3>Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle</h3>
<p>Experience, reflection, concept, experiment, and around again. Learning comes from doing and thinking about what happened. <strong>Use it</strong> for leadership development and learning on the job. <strong>Avoid it</strong> when there is no real time for reflection; a rushed cycle loses most of its value.</p>

<h3>4C/ID (Four-Component Instructional Design)</h3>
<p>Whole, realistic tasks supported by four components that build complex skills step by step. It is grounded in cognitive load research. <strong>Use it</strong> for long programs that build complex professional skills over weeks or months. <strong>Avoid it</strong> for a quick, specific need; the mapping effort only pays off on long programs.</p>

<h3>ARCS model of motivation</h3>
<p>Attention, Relevance, Confidence and Satisfaction: four conditions that keep learners engaged. ARCS is a lens, not a content structure. <strong>Use it</strong> on top of another model, especially when engagement or completion is low. <strong>Avoid it</strong> as the only structure of a course.</p>

<h3>Scenario-based learning</h3>
<p>Learners step into a realistic situation, make decisions and see the consequences. <strong>Use it</strong> for judgment calls: sales, service, compliance, leadership. <strong>Avoid it</strong> for simple facts or procedures, where there is no real decision to practice.</p>

<h3>Problem-based learning</h3>
<p>Small groups get a complex, open problem before they have all the theory, and build the knowledge to solve it with a facilitator. <strong>Use it</strong> for roles that diagnose and solve messy problems every day. <strong>Avoid it</strong> when time is short and the content is factual and well defined.</p>

<h3>Project-based learning</h3>
<p>People learn over a longer period by producing a real deliverable, often for a real audience. <strong>Use it</strong> for broad skills such as collaboration, creativity and project management. <strong>Avoid it</strong> without checkpoints and feedback along the way, or it turns into a last-minute rush.</p>

<h3>Flipped classroom</h3>
<p>People study the content before the live session, and the session is used for practice, discussion and questions. <strong>Use it</strong> when live time is scarce and valuable. <strong>Avoid it</strong> when learners cannot reliably do the pre-work.</p>

<h3>Peer-to-peer learning</h3>
<p>Colleagues teach, give feedback and mentor each other. <strong>Use it</strong> to reinforce what was taught and spread know-how inside teams. <strong>Avoid it</strong> as the only source for critical technical or regulatory content, where one wrong explanation can spread unchecked.</p>

<h2 id="idm-choose">How to choose: four questions</h2>

<p>Answer these before you open a blank page. Most requests point to one or two models by the third question.</p>

<ol class="pg-list"><li><strong>Is this one stuck step, or a whole skill?</strong> One step where performance stalls, already shown by data: ReFrame Learning. A complex skill built over months: 4C/ID. A single module with a clear objective: Gagné.</li><li><strong>Does the job require judgment?</strong> If people must decide in ambiguous situations, practice the decision: scenario-based or problem-based learning. If it is a fixed procedure, a clear sequence works better.</li><li><strong>How much live time do you have?</strong> Little and valuable: flip it, and use the session for practice. Time to reflect between sessions: Kolb. A real output expected at the end: project-based.</li><li><strong>Is the problem knowledge or motivation?</strong> If people know what to do and still do not do it, add ARCS on top of the model you chose. If the knowledge already lives in the team, spread it peer to peer.</li></ol>

<p>Whatever you pick, check it against Merrill's First Principles before you build: a real task at the center, a demonstration, practice and application on the job.</p>

<h2 id="idm-mistakes">Common mistakes when choosing a model</h2>

<ul class="pg-list"><li><strong>Using one model for everything.</strong> A team that only knows Gagné turns every request into a nine-step module, even a two-minute fix.</li><li><strong>Building a full program for a single stuck step.</strong> When one step is the problem, a long course buries it.</li><li><strong>Treating motivation as a content problem.</strong> More content does not fix low engagement; a motivation lens like ARCS does.</li><li><strong>Teaching judgment with slides.</strong> Decisions are learned by deciding. Without scenarios or problems, people know the rule and still get it wrong.</li><li><strong>Flipping without pre-work.</strong> If learners arrive without the content, the live session goes back to being a lecture.</li><li><strong>Choosing after drafting.</strong> Picking the model once the content exists means reorganizing everything. Choose first, then write.</li></ul>
