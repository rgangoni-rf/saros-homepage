<h2 id="lo-week">Where did the week go?</h2>

<p>Monday starts with a Slack message: “Can we get a quick training on the new CRM by Friday?” There is no goal attached, no audience size, no deadline that anyone has agreed to. By Wednesday you have chased two approvals, rebuilt a status spreadsheet for a VP and answered the same question about a compliance course three times. By Friday the course is half-built, and nobody has asked what it is supposed to change.</p>

<p>If that week sounds familiar, the problem is rarely your instructional design. It is the system around it. Learning teams have spent two decades getting very good at building learning, and much less time designing how the work itself arrives, moves and gets measured. That second job has a name: learning operations.</p>
<h2 id="lo-definition">What is learning operations?</h2>

<p class="lo-def"><strong>Learning operations is the discipline of running L&amp;D as a system: how training requests come in, how they are prioritized, how the work moves from design to delivery, and how results are reported against the business goal that started it.</strong></p>

<p>If instructional design answers “how should people learn this?”, learning operations answers everything around it: Who asked? Why? Is training even the right answer? Who owns the next step? Where is the project stuck? Did it work?</p>

<p>The idea is not new. Sales has sales operations, marketing has marketing operations, and IT has had IT service management for decades. Each one appeared when a function grew too large to run on goodwill and inboxes. L&amp;D has reached that point. ATD’s TD magazine has even published a guide to building a learning operations blueprint (<a href="https://www.td.org/magazines/td-magazine/the-blueprint-for-developing-a-learning-operations-blueprint" rel="noopener" target="_blank">TD magazine</a>).</p>
<h2 id="lo-why-now">Why learning operations matters now</h2>

<p>L&amp;D is being asked to do more with less, and the numbers say so plainly. In ATD’s 2026 State of the Industry report, formal learning hours rose from 13.7 in 2024 to 16.7 in 2025, while direct learning expenditure per employee fell from $1,254 to $846 (<a href="https://www.td.org/content/atd-blog/2026-state-of-the-industry-report-indicates-latest-td-trends" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ATD</a>). More learning, less money per person: that gap is closed by how well the work is run, not by working longer.</p><figure class="lo-stats"><div><b>13.7 → 16.7</b><span>formal learning hours, 2024 to 2025</span></div>

<div><b>$1,254 → $846</b><span>direct learning spend per employee</span></div><figcaption>Source: <a href="https://www.td.org/content/atd-blog/2026-state-of-the-industry-report-indicates-latest-td-trends" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ATD 2026 State of the Industry</a></figcaption></figure><p>The same report shows where the pressure lands. Prioritizing multiple demands is among the top three challenges for 46% of talent development teams, and scaling with few resources follows at 33% (<a href="https://www.td.org/content/atd-blog/2026-state-of-the-industry-report-indicates-latest-td-trends" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ATD</a>). Neither is a content problem. Both are operating problems.</p>

<p>Underneath, three pains show up again and again:</p>

<ul class="pg-list"><li><strong>Requests arrive half-written.</strong> “We need a training” comes with no goal, no audience and no measure of success. The team spends its first week on detective work.</li><li><strong>Nobody can see the whole pipeline.</strong> Projects live across a task board, a spreadsheet and a dozen email threads. Asking “what is late, and why?” means asking around.</li><li><strong>Results stop at attendance.</strong> Only 37% of HR managers count business impact among the ways they measure training success (<a href="https://www.talentlms.com/research/learning-development-report-2026" rel="noopener" target="_blank">TalentLMS</a>). Completion rates are easy to report; they are rarely what the business asked for.</li></ul>

<p>Meanwhile the tools are already there. 89% of US organizations use a learning management system, and US training spend reached $102.8 billion in 2025 (<a href="https://trainingmag.com/2025-training-industry-report/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Training Magazine</a>). The LMS was built to deliver and track learning. The work that happens before and after delivery usually has no home.</p>
<h2 id="lo-pillars">The five pillars of learning operations</h2>

<p>Every mature learning operation rests on five pillars. You do not need all five on day one, but you need to know which one is missing.</p>

<h3>1. Intake</h3>

<p>A single front door for training requests. A good intake asks the business for the problem, the audience, the deadline and, above all, the KPI the training is meant to move (we cover the nine <a href="#blog-training-intake-form-template-questions">training intake questions</a> in a separate guide). It also asks the uncomfortable question early: is training the right answer, or is this a process, tool or incentive problem?</p>

<h3>2. Prioritization</h3>

<p>Not every request deserves a course. A clear scoring model (impact, audience size, risk, readiness, effort) turns “who shouted loudest” into a decision the business can see and accept. The backlog stops being a secret and becomes a shared agreement.</p>

<h3>3. Workflow</h3>

<p>Once a project is approved, it moves through visible stages: assessment, design, development, engagement and results. Each stage has an owner and a clock. When something stalls, you see it in days, not at the quarterly review.</p>

<h3>4. Capacity</h3>

<p>Your designers, facilitators, subject matter experts and vendors are finite. Capacity planning means knowing who is working on what, what is coming next and when to say “not this quarter” with data instead of apologies.</p>

<h3>5. Measurement</h3>

<p>The loop closes where it started. The KPI captured at intake becomes the metric in the results report, compared with the baseline. That is how L&amp;D stops reporting activity and starts reporting impact.</p>
<h2 id="lo-vs-lms">Learning operations vs. your LMS vs. a project tool</h2>

<p>Learning operations does not replace your LMS. It covers the work the LMS was never designed to hold.</p>

<div class="tbl"><table><thead><tr><th></th><th><strong>LMS</strong></th><th><strong>Generic project tool</strong></th><th><strong>Learning operations</strong></th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Main question</td><td>Who completed what?</td><td>Which tasks are done?</td><td>Did the training do what the business asked?</td></tr><tr><td>Starts at</td><td>Enrollment</td><td>A task someone creates</td><td>The business request and its KPI</td></tr><tr><td>Ends at</td><td>Completion and scores</td><td>A closed ticket</td><td>The result, compared with the baseline</td></tr><tr><td>Built for</td><td>Learners and admins</td><td>Any team</td><td>Requesters, L&amp;D, designers, instructors and learners</td></tr><tr><td>Blind spot</td><td>Everything before launch</td><td>The shape of a training project</td><td>Delivery itself: it relies on your LMS for that</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>A task board can tell you a storyboard is done. It cannot tell you that the same onboarding request has come in from four regions this year, or that half your projects wait more than two weeks for business sign-off. That pattern is the operation talking.</p>
<h2 id="lo-metrics">Metrics that show your operation is working</h2>

<p>You cannot improve an operation you cannot see. Start with a handful of measures that describe the flow of work, not just the learners.</p>

<div class="tbl"><table><thead><tr><th><strong>Metric</strong></th><th><strong>What it tells you</strong></th><th><strong>Where it comes from</strong></th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Request-to-decision time</td><td>How long the business waits for a yes, no or alternative</td><td>Intake and triage</td></tr><tr><td>Share of requests redirected</td><td>How often training was not the right answer</td><td>Triage outcomes</td></tr><tr><td>Days in stage</td><td>Where projects stall, and for how long</td><td>Workflow</td></tr><tr><td>On-time delivery rate</td><td>Whether commitments to the business hold</td><td>Workflow</td></tr><tr><td>Backlog age</td><td>How long approved work waits before it starts</td><td>Prioritization</td></tr><tr><td>Capacity load</td><td>Who is over- or under-committed this quarter</td><td>Capacity</td></tr><tr><td>KPI movement vs. baseline</td><td>Whether the training changed what the business cared about</td><td>Measurement</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>The last metric is the one leadership remembers. The others are how you get there reliably.</p>
<h2 id="lo-90-days">How to start in 90 days</h2>

<p>You do not need a reorganization to begin. You need one front door, one shared view and one honest report.</p>

<div class="lo-phases"><div><span>Days 1–30</span><h4>Open the front door</h4>

<ul><li>Write down every open request and project in one place, even if it is a spreadsheet.</li><li>Publish a short intake form that asks for the problem, the audience, the deadline and the KPI. A <a href="#templates-training-intake-form">free training intake form template</a> is a good starting point.</li><li>Pilot it with one or two business areas that already trust you.</li></ul></div>

<div><span>Days 31–60</span><h4>Make the work visible</h4>

<ul><li>Agree on five to seven stages, from request to results, and give each one an owner.</li><li>Score new requests with a simple model and share the backlog with your stakeholders.</li><li>Start tracking days in stage. The first stall you catch early will sell the idea for you.</li></ul></div>

<div><span>Days 61–90</span><h4>Close the loop</h4>

<ul><li>Pick one finished project and report its result against the KPI and baseline from intake.</li><li>Review your first numbers: request-to-decision time, on-time delivery, backlog age.</li><li>Decide what to automate next, based on where the most time was lost.</li></ul></div></div>

<p>By day 90 you will not have a perfect operation. You will have something more useful: evidence of where your team’s time actually goes. If you want a baseline before you start, the <a href="#health-check">L&amp;D Operating Health Check</a> maps it in about seven minutes.</p>
<h2 id="lo-night-sky">A curiosity from the night sky</h2>

<div class="lo-saros"><p>Long before anyone talked about data, Babylonian astronomers kept careful records of eclipses. Over generations, those records revealed a pattern: similar eclipses return roughly every 18 years and 11 days. Today that cycle is called the saros. They did not predict the sky by guessing. They predicted it by paying attention to what kept coming back.</p>

<p>L&amp;D has its own saros. The onboarding course requested again by a new region. The compliance project that always stalls in the same approval. The skill gap that surfaces every year, just before a product launch. Each one looks like a one-off when it lands in an inbox. Recorded in one place, they become a pattern you can plan for.</p>

<p>That idea is where the name of our platform, Saros, comes from. It is also, quietly, the whole case for learning operations: write down every request, follow it to its result, and the patterns start to show.</p></div>
