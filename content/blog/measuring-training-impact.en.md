<p>Completion rates do not show impact. Learn how to measure training impact with one business KPI: pick it before, record a baseline, set a target, compare.</p>

<h2 id="mti-gap">Completions are not impact</h2>

<p>A training report that says "94% completed" answers the wrong question. Leadership does not ask who finished the course. It asks what changed in the business because of it.</p>

<p>Most L&amp;D teams know this and still report completions, because completions are the number the LMS gives them for free. Impact takes a decision made much earlier: which business number the training should move, and what that number was before anyone started.</p>

<p>The gap is wide. Only 37% of HR managers count business impact among the ways they measure training success, according to the <a href="https://www.talentlms.com/research/learning-development-report-2026" rel="noopener" target="_blank">TalentLMS 2026 L&amp;D Benchmark Report</a>. The rest stop at attendance, completion and satisfaction scores.</p>

<p>This guide shows how to close that gap with one business KPI per training request: what the classic evaluation models get right, where they stall in practice, and a four-step approach any L&amp;D team can start on its next request.</p>

<h2 id="mti-models">Kirkpatrick and Phillips in brief</h2>

<p>Two models shape almost every conversation about training evaluation. Both are useful. Both stall at the same place in real projects.</p>

<div class="tbl"><table><thead><tr><th><strong>Level</strong></th><th><strong>Kirkpatrick question</strong></th><th><strong>What teams usually measure</strong></th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>1. Reaction</strong></td><td>Did people find it useful and engaging?</td><td>Satisfaction survey after the session</td></tr><tr><td><strong>2. Learning</strong></td><td>Did they gain the knowledge and skills?</td><td>Quiz or assessment scores</td></tr><tr><td><strong>3. Behavior</strong></td><td>Do they do things differently on the job?</td><td>Manager observation, often skipped</td></tr><tr><td><strong>4. Results</strong></td><td>Did the business outcome move?</td><td>Rarely measured</td></tr><tr><td><strong>5. ROI (Phillips)</strong></td><td>Was the benefit worth the cost?</td><td>Rarely measured</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p><strong>Kirkpatrick's four levels</strong> were introduced by Donald Kirkpatrick in 1959. Their strength is the chain: reaction leads to learning, learning to behavior, behavior to results. <strong>Phillips ROI</strong> adds a fifth level that turns the result into money and compares it with the program's cost.</p>

<p><strong>Where they stall.</strong> Both models are usually applied after the training ends. By then, nobody recorded which business number the training was meant to move, or where it stood before. Level 4 becomes a guess, and level 5 a spreadsheet nobody trusts. The fix is not a better survey at the end. It is a decision at the start.</p>

<h2 id="mti-steps">The one-KPI approach in four steps</h2>

<p>Measuring impact is mostly decided before the training exists. These four steps fit inside a normal training request and take minutes, not months.</p>

<ol class="pg-list"><li><strong>Choose one business KPI before you design.</strong> Ask the requester which number will move if the training works: time to productivity, error rate, conversion, customer satisfaction, safety incidents. One KPI beats five. If nobody can name one, the request is not ready yet.</li><li><strong>Record the baseline.</strong> Write down today's value and the system or report it comes from, so you can pull the same number again later. A target without a starting point is a guess.</li><li><strong>Agree on a target and a date.</strong> "From 16 weeks to 10 weeks by the end of Q3" turns a wish into a commitment both sides can check. Agree on it with the business, not inside L&amp;D.</li><li><strong>Compare after rollout, by group.</strong> Pull the same KPI from the same source and compare it with the baseline, by cohort, region or channel. Groups that trained earlier, or not yet, give you a fair point of comparison without a formal study.</li></ol>

<p class="lo-def"><strong>Example (sample data).</strong> A contact center asks for onboarding training. KPI: time to productivity. Baseline: 16 weeks. Target: 10 weeks by Q3. After two cohorts, trained agents reach productivity in 11 weeks; agents hired before the program still take 15. The report shows the KPI the business chose, not a completion rate.</p>

<p>When the KPI does not move, that is a result too. It points to a cause training cannot fix alone, such as a tool, a process or a staffing gap, and it tells you where to look next.</p>

<h2 id="mti-mistakes">Common mistakes when measuring training impact</h2>

<ul class="pg-list"><li><strong>Choosing the KPI after the training.</strong> Any number picked at the end looks like it was picked to fit. Decide it in the request.</li><li><strong>Measuring five KPIs.</strong> Several numbers dilute the story and invite cherry-picking. Pick one; mention others only as context.</li><li><strong>Using a KPI nobody owns.</strong> If the business does not already track the number, you cannot pull it again. Use a number from an existing report or system.</li><li><strong>Skipping the baseline.</strong> Without the starting value, even a real improvement cannot be shown.</li><li><strong>Claiming the whole change.</strong> Other things move business numbers too. Compare trained and not-yet-trained groups and say what else changed in the period.</li><li><strong>Reporting once and stopping.</strong> Behavior and results take weeks to show. Check the KPI again after 30, 60 and 90 days.</li></ul>
